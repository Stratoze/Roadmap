# Musicianship
>
> Musicianship is not a supplement to piano study. It is piano study.
> Sight-reading, ear training, theory, and listening are co-equal pillars with repertoire and technique.
> Design principle: every session should engage at least two of the four pillars:
> Visual (reading), Aural (ear), Motor (hands), Analytical (mind).

## Sight-Reading

Use music easy enough to continue without stopping, roughly 2 levels below current.

### Cognitive architecture of sight-reading

Sight-reading is not one skill but a cascade:

1. Visual recognition: notes, intervals, patterns
2. Auditory prediction: hearing what comes next before playing it
3. Motor execution: translating prediction into finger movement
4. Error monitoring: detecting mismatches between prediction and output
5. Recovery: continuing despite errors without stopping

Training must address all five levels, not just note recognition.

### Before

- scan key, meter, rhythm, hand positions, accidentals
- identify patterns: scales, arpeggios, sequences, repeated figures
- choose a pulse
- hear the opening internally (auditory mapping)
- sing or hum the first phrase before playing

### During

- keep moving
- no going back
- prioritize pulse, rhythm, bass, and melodic direction
- simplify a dense chord rather than stopping
- look ahead: eyes 2–4 beats ahead of hands
- chunk notes into groups, not individual notes
- trust harmonic prediction: if you miss a note, the harmony tells you what it likely was

### After

- identify 1–2 recurring reading problems
- do not convert the sight-reading piece into repertoire practice
- usually do not replay
- note which patterns were recognized instantly vs decoded slowly

### Eye-hand span training

The eye-hand span is the distance between where your eyes are and where your hands are playing.
Larger span = more time to process = smoother reading.

Exercises:

1. **Look-ahead drill**: Play a simple piece while a partner or metronome forces you to keep going. Eyes must stay ahead.
2. **Repeated reading**: Play a short unfamiliar piece 3 times. Observe how eye movements change. First reading is decoding; second is pattern recognition; third is anticipation.
3. **Peripheral awareness**: While playing, try to perceive the next measure without directly looking at it. This trains peripheral vision.
4. **Blind intervals**: Play a short passage while looking 2 measures ahead. Trust your hands.

### Dual-task training for sight-reading

Sight-reading requires managing multiple cognitive streams simultaneously.
Dual-task training builds this capacity.

Start simple, increase difficulty:

1. Play a piece while counting aloud
2. Play while naming the note letters
3. Play while naming the chord (I, IV, V, vi)
4. Play while conducting with the free hand
5. Play while counting a different subdivision
6. Play while analyzing harmony aloud
7. Play while listening for a specific voice

Only add dual-task when the base reading is at 80% accuracy.

### Pattern recognition curriculum

Train these patterns until they are instant:

- Stage 0–1: five-finger positions, steps and skips, simple rhythms
- Stage 2–3: scales, arpeggios, Alberti bass, I-IV-V-I
- Stage 4–5: sequences, diminished chords, chromatic passages, ii-V-I
- Stage 6–7: complex harmonies, modern notation, orchestral reductions
- Stage 8–9: prima vista professional repertoire

## Ear, Theory and Functional Playing

Rotate rather than doing everything daily.

### Ear training progression

| Stage | Focus | Exercises |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0–1 | Intervals, major/minor | Sing and identify: M2, M3, P5. Major vs minor triad. |
| 2–3 | Chords, progressions | Identify I, IV, V, vi. Sing chord tones. Transcribe 4-bar melodies. |
| 4–5 | Seventh chords, extensions | Identify maj7, m7, dom7, dim7. Transcribe 8-bar melodies with bass. |
| 6–7 | Complex harmony | Identify secondary dominants, modal interchange. Transcribe full songs. |
| 8–9 | Advanced ear | Transcribe by ear in real time. Identify voicings, extensions, alterations. |

### Weekly ear training minimum

- 2–3 five-minute sessions per week
- plus one 10-minute chord, by-ear, or lead-sheet session per week

### Ear training activities (rotate)

- sing the melody before playing it
- clap or count rhythms
- identify intervals and chord qualities
- name scale degrees, find cadences
- analyze 4 measures harmonically
- transpose a short phrase
- harmonize a melody
- play from a lead sheet
- learn 4 measures by ear
- improvise in the current key or chord progression

### Transcription protocol

Transcription is the bridge between ear training and functional playing.

Progressive curriculum:

1. **Melody only**: Find the melody note by note. Sing it first.
2. **Melody + bass**: Add the bass line or root of each chord.
3. **Melody + chords**: Name the progression. Play melody with block chords.
4. **Full arrangement**: Add comping pattern, intro, outro.
5. **Animenz level**: Transcribe complex piano arrangements with multiple voices.

Rules:

- Always sing before you play
- Work in short phrases: 2–4 bars at a time
- Check against the recording after each phrase
- Do not look at sheet music until you have tried by ear first
- Log what you transcribed in your daily note

### Dual-task training for ear playing

- Play a chord progression while singing a melody
- Play a melody while naming the chords
- Play bass notes while clapping the rhythm
- Play a song while counting subdivisions aloud

### Resources

- Functional Ear Trainer, free
- musictheory.net, free
- Perfect Ear, free
- YouTube: anime songs for transcription practice
- Animenz arrangements for advanced transcription study

## Listening Habit

Listening is not optional enrichment. It is part of learning and polishing.

### Minimum listening requirements

For current learning piece:

- Listen to 1–2 recordings before the first practice session.
- Follow the score if possible.
- During the first week, listen once more with the score while identifying:
  - tempo
  - phrase shape
  - articulation
  - character
  - difficult spots

For current polishing piece:

- Do one comparison listen every 1–2 weeks.
- Use 2–3 different artists if possible.
- Listen with one question only:
  - How do they voice the melody?
  - How do they pedal?
  - Where do they breathe?
  - What is their tempo relationship?
  - What character do they project?

For repertoire one level above:

- Optional, 1 focused listen per week.
- Use commute, dishes, or before bed.
- Score-following when possible.

### Listening rule

Active listening with a specific question beats passive listening.
Do not use background listening as a substitute for score study.

### Listening for ear-playing development

- Listen to anime/J-pop songs actively: identify the progression, form, and key
- Listen to Animenz recordings and follow along with the score if available
- Listen to multiple pianists playing the same song: compare voicing, rubato, and arrangement choices
- Listen to jazz and classical to expand harmonic vocabulary

## Mental Practice and Score Study

Mental practice is a legitimate training modality, not a substitute but a complement.

### When to use

- Travel days, rest days, injury days
- Before bed (consolidation window)
- Between physical practice sessions
- When physically fatigued but mentally alert

### Protocol

1. **Visualize**: See the keys, feel the movements, hear the sound. Be specific.
2. **Score study**: Trace the harmony, mark the form, hear it internally.
3. **Table practice**: "Play" on a table or your lap, feeling finger movements.
4. **Singing**: Sing or hum a voice from the piece.
5. **Analytical**: Describe the harmony, form, and structure in words.

### Quality matters

The more sensory detail — exact fingers, dynamics, key feel, sound — the more useful.
Vague thinking about piano does not count.
Passive listening while doing dishes does not count.

### Neuroscience basis

Mental practice activates the same motor cortex regions as physical practice.
It strengthens neural pathways without physical wear.
It is especially effective for:

- Memorization security
- Passage planning and fingering decisions
- Recovery from injury
- Consolidation during sleep (practice visualization before bed)
