# Practice Protocols

> Reference document. Consult during weekly reviews, troubleshooting, or when choosing new material.

> [!abstract] The rules that matter most
>
> 1. It's a marathon, not a sprint.
> 2. Interleave: rotate material every 5–8 minutes during learning/polishing.
> 3. Rule of Almost: leave passages almost secure, then return later.
> 4. Slow is fast: speed increases only when control is present.
> 5. End clean: the last repetition should be one you would happily repeat tomorrow.
> 6. Sleep consolidates: practice timing matters.
> 7. Body first: biomechanical efficiency prevents injury and enables speed.


> [!warning] Health red flags
> Stop immediately for pain, weakness, tingling, numbness, swelling, loss of dexterity, unusual fatigue, or increasing tension. See [Technique and Health](#technique-and-health).

## Session Engine

### Minimum rules expanded

These are the expanded version of the daily note rules (see `_system/Daily Template.md`).

- Sit down. Play something. Even one scale counts.
- Work on ONE thing with full attention.
- After 3 identical reps, change something.
- Stuck on a spot? 5–10 min max, then rotate.
- Tired? Do the minimum. No guilt.
- End clean, not degraded.

### Session Options

Not a fixed schedule; pick based on energy and time.

#### Minimum, 15–25 min

1. Move gently, sit down, play something easy, 2–3 min.
2. Most important current chunk, focused work, 8–12 min.
3. One maintenance review or one sight-read, 3–5 min.
4. Play something fun, 2 min.

#### Standard, 40–60 min, interleaved

The order below is a default. Rotate the middle blocks across sessions.
Within learning and polishing blocks, alternate between 2–3 chunks or pieces every 5–8 minutes rather than grinding one passage for 18 straight minutes.

1. Warm-up and physical check, 3–5 min.
2. Technique: etude, scales, or arpeggios by objective, 8–12 min.
3. Learning block A: first chunk of learning piece, 6–8 min.
4. Interleave: sight-reading, ear drill, or maintenance review, 5 min.
5. Learning block B: second chunk or different piece, 6–8 min.
6. Polishing piece: musical and performance work, 8–10 min.
7. Fun or play-by-ear, 2–5 min.
8. Journal line and log links, 1–2 min.

Interleaving feels worse during the session, but retention is better. Blocking produces fast session-level gains that evaporate.

#### Long session

Divide into two or more blocks with a real break.
Never grind one passage for 30 minutes.
Include a biomechanical check-in at the midpoint: scan for tension, check wrist alignment, release shoulders.

### Weekly targets

These are aims, not obligations.

- practice 5–6 days
- at least 1 light or rest day
- 4–6 repertoire sessions
- 3–5 technique sessions
- 3–5 sight-reading sessions, 5 min each
- 2–3 ear and theory sessions, 5 min each
- 1 chord, lead-sheet, improv, or by-ear session
- 1 recorded performance take
- 2–3 focused listening sessions
- 1 brief weekly review
- 1 mental practice or score study session (can replace a physical session)

### Sleep and timing

- Your brain consolidates motor skills between sessions, especially during sleep.
- Daily practice plus good sleep beats weekend marathons.
- If you can choose, practicing closer to bedtime gives sleep more material to consolidate.
- Protect sleep duration. A 45-minute session followed by 8 hours of sleep outperforms a 2-hour session followed by 5 hours.
- Naps or brief rest after practice may also help.
- After practice, spend 2–3 minutes visualizing the passage you worked on. This primes consolidation.
- Do not practice intensely within 30 minutes of sleep if it causes arousal. Gentle review or mental practice is fine.

### Sleep consolidation protocol

1. Identify the 1–2 most important passages from today's session.
2. Before bed, spend 3–5 minutes visualizing playing those passages perfectly.
3. See the keys, feel the movements, hear the sound. Be specific.
4. Do not physically play. This is mental rehearsal only.
5. Sleep 7–9 hours. The brain replays and consolidates during slow-wave sleep.
6. Next morning, test the passage cold. Expect improvement of 10–20%.

### Mental practice away from the piano

Mental practice is not a substitute for physical practice, but it is a legitimate supplement.
Use it on travel days, rest days, injury days, or before bed.

What counts:

- Visualize playing a passage: see the keys, feel the movements, hear the sound.
- Study the score away from the piano.
- Trace the harmony, mark the form, hear it internally.
- "Play" on a table or your lap, feeling finger movements without sound.
- Sing or hum a voice from the piece.
- Listen to a recording while following the score with a specific question.

What does not count:

- Passive listening while doing dishes.
- Vague anxious thinking about piano.

Quality matters. The more sensory detail — exact fingers, dynamics, key feel, sound — the more useful it is.

## Learning Protocol

### Step 1: Preview and mandatory listening

Before touching the keys, spend about 2–5 minutes:

- key, time signature, form, phrase structure
- character and intended sound
- difficult rhythms, fingering decisions, pedaling issues
- target tempo or tempo range
- mark the 2–3 hardest spots as Q-spots
- sing or hum the main melody

Mandatory listening:

- Before the first practice session on a new piece, listen to 1–2 recordings while following the score.
- If you cannot follow the score yet, listen once without the score, then scan the score.
- Do not begin note-learning cold unless no recording exists.

If you cannot sing the main melody, you have not heard it clearly enough.

### Step 2: Define the chunk

Use the smallest musically useful unit:

- one motive
- one beat
- one measure
- 2–4 measures
- one short phrase

Go smaller when:

- fingering is uncertain
- the same error repeats
- coordination breaks down
- tension appears

### Step 3: Choose the hand strategy

HS is not mandatory.
Use HS when:

- one hand has a difficult pattern on its own
- fingering is unclear
- you need to study voicing or articulation in one hand
- the left hand is being ignored

Use HT when:

- the coordination itself is the skill
- the passage uses symmetrical or interdependent movement
- HS feels easy but combining always fails
- the texture is already simple

Useful pattern:
very slow HT to understand coordination → HS to solve individual problems → HT again immediately.

### Step 4: Establish sound, fingering, rhythm, and musicality from day 1

Before increasing speed:

- count or clap difficult rhythms
- sing the principal voice
- choose fingering intentionally
- decide articulation, phrase direction, dynamics, character
- play slowly enough to monitor physical ease and intended sound

The intended sound helps determine the movement.
Do not automate a mechanically correct but musically blank version.
The metronome is a check tool, not a constant companion. Use it after rhythm and movement are understood. Overuse flattens expression.

### Step 5: Repetition with error triage and Rule of Almost

After each repetition, name one observation. Then respond:

| Situation | Response |
| --- | --- |
| Isolated slip | Note it, repeat once. Not a crisis. |
| Same mistake twice | Stop. Identify the cause: fingering? reading? rhythm? movement? anticipation? chunk too big? Change the method before repeating. |
| Hesitation | Start one beat or measure earlier. Include the transitions in and out. |
| Tension or discomfort | Stop immediately. Reduce speed or dynamic. Reconsider movement and fingering. |
| Major breakdown | Shrink the chunk. Change strategy entirely. |

After 3 identical repetitions, deliberately change something:

- dynamics
- articulation
- tempo
- hands
- character
- rhythm

Repeating on autopilot stops producing learning.

#### Rule of Almost

Do not grind a passage to perfection before moving on.

- Work a chunk until it is almost secure — roughly 70–80% reliable.
- Switch to another chunk, piece, or activity.
- Return later and attempt it cold before looking.
- The struggle to recall is part of the learning.

This applies to memorization too:
attempt from memory first, then check the score.
Easy recall means you are reviewing. Effortful recall means you are building.

### Step 6: Join chunks with overlap

Practice the seams:

- end of A plus beginning of B
- last beat of A plus first beat of B
- last measure of A plus first measure of B

After joining, reduce tempo by whatever amount makes the joined section feel controlled.

### Step 7: Stop, return, and log

After 5–10 minutes on the same problem:

- switch passage
- switch piece
- take a break
- stop for the day

Return later and test whether the solution survived.
Results appear later, not immediately. Today's work may surface tomorrow, next week, or in two weeks.

If an error appears across multiple sessions:

1. Add it to [Error Logs](../Logs/Error%20Logs.md).
2. If it belongs to one piece, move it into that piece's error log.
3. Choose one strategy, not five.
4. Retest next session.

## Tempo and Speed

### Adaptive Tempo Protocol

#### Set the goal

Use a target tempo range, not a single sacred number.
Sources, in order of preference:

1. composer's or editor's metronome marking
2. RCM technical requirement
3. musical character and physical control
4. several reputable performances as references, not commands
5. teacher feedback

#### Starting tempo

Start at the fastest tempo at which you can still control:

- notes
- rhythm
- fingering
- articulation
- intended sound
- physical ease

That might be 40%, 70%, or 90% of target. Do not default to 50 BPM.

#### Advancement

At the current tempo, play up to 3 times:

| Result | Action |
| --- | --- |
| 2–3 controlled repetitions | Increase slightly |
| One isolated slip, otherwise good control | Diagnose it, repeat at the same tempo |
| Same error twice, major hesitation, or tension | Reduce meaningfully. Shrink the chunk or change the method. |

The real rule: advance when your ears and hands report control; retreat when they report loss of control.

#### Tempo testing

Use some combination of:

- a comfortable slow tempo
- an intermediate tempo
- the target tempo
- slightly varied tempos
- one cold target-tempo attempt on another day

You do not need a rigid pass formula for every piece.

### Speed Work

#### Prerequisite

Speed work is a second-stage tool.
Apply it only to material whose notes, fingering, and movements are already secure at slow tempo.
Slow mastery first, then speed work.

#### Why slow practice alone is not enough

Slow playing uses different movement patterns than fast playing.
You can be accurate at 60 BPM and fall apart at 120 because the physical coordination at speed is different.
Speed must be practiced directly, in small controlled bursts.

### Chaining

For genuinely fast passages:

1. Play 1 measure or one beat-group at full tempo. Stop. Reset. Repeat.
2. Chain: 1 measure → 2 measures → 4 measures at tempo.
3. Use brief bursts slightly above target initially. Stop if quality deteriorates.
4. Build chain length gradually.

Counterbalance: too much speed-only practice degrades motor control and finesse. Return to slow work periodically.
Chaining is not a shortcut past slow practice. It is a late-stage addition.
Not required for lyrical, slow, beginner, or already-tense material.

### Rhythm variations

For even-note passages that will not come out even.

1. Dotted: LONG-short, LONG-short
   - on the LONG note: actively release tension
   - on the short notes: light, fast, lean into the next long note
   - the physical release between notes is the purpose
2. Reverse dotted: short-LONG, short-LONG
3. Grouped: 3+1, 2+2, 1+3

Use after the tempo protocol stalls on a specific passage.
Do not use on already-musical or rubato passages.

### Variable Practice

Once a passage is secure at slow tempo, vary the conditions deliberately.
This builds a more flexible motor schema than repeating the same version endlessly.

Variation menu — pick 2–3 per session, not all:

- Dynamics: play the passage pp, then ff, then with a crescendo.
- Articulation: legato → staccato → portato → mixed.
- Tempo: slow → medium → one burst at target → back to slow.
- Starting point: begin at beat 2, or at the second measure, or at the climax.
- Rhythm: dotted, reverse-dotted, grouped.
- Hands: HT → HS on the harder hand → HT.
- Character: play it as a lullaby, then as a march, then as a question.
- Transposition: up or down a step, advanced.

Use variable practice when:

- the learning protocol has established notes, fingering, and basic shape;
- a passage feels stuck at a certain tempo;
- preparing for performance.

Do not use variable practice when:

- it is day one of learning;
- you are already fatigued;
- the passage is still unclear.

### Ending the session

End in control and quality.

- After accuracy or memorization work: one calm, slow, clean run-through.
- After chaining or speed work: if quality is still good, ending at-speed is fine. If quality is degrading, use one calm slower repetition.
- Never end a fast passage in a degraded or tense state.
- Never end a session mid-breakdown.
- The last repetition should be one you would be happy to repeat tomorrow.
- After the last repetition, spend 30 seconds visualizing the passage perfectly. This primes sleep consolidation.

## Problem Solving

### Q-Spots

For persistent problem areas. From Graham Fitch's Practising the Piano.

1. Identify: mark the exact bars. Bracket them in the score.
2. Isolate: work only those bars with the specific tool:
   - rhythm problem → rhythm variations
   - speed problem → chaining
   - coordination → HS, then shadow practice
   - memory → transpose, one-finger, wrong hand, eyes closed
   - tension → reduce tempo, check biomechanics, release shoulders
3. Time-box: 5–8 minutes per Q-spot. Then rotate.
4. Revisit: come back the next day. And the next.
5. Reintegrate: phrase → section → whole piece.
6. Keep them on active pieces: even after a piece enters maintenance, revisit former Q-spots periodically.

### Plateau-Breaking

Use when stuck for 2+ weeks. Pick 1–2, not all.

- Cut tempo dramatically. Rebuild from glacial speed.
- Apply rhythm variations to the stuck passage.
- Practice HS what you always play HT, or vice versa.
- Change octave, dynamic, or articulation.
- Take 2–3 days off that piece. Come back fresh.
- Play something in a different style or era for a week.
- Post a recording to r/piano, Piano World, or Piano Street.
- Tackle a stretch piece temporarily for focus and motivation.
- Get a teacher's perspective, even one Zoom lesson.
- Shelve honestly and return in a month.
- Check biomechanics: is the movement efficient? Is there hidden tension?

### Recurring error workflow

Use this when the same error keeps returning.

1. Capture it in [Error Logs](../Logs/Error%20Logs.md).
2. If it belongs to a specific piece, move it into that piece's error log.
3. Mark the exact bar(s).
4. Name the error type:
   - fingering
   - reading
   - rhythm
   - coordination
   - memory
   - tension
   - anticipation
   - biomechanical (inefficient movement, wrong joint)
5. Choose one strategy.
6. Retest next session, not immediately after ten failed attempts.

If the same error appears three sessions in a row, it is data. It means the strategy is wrong, not that you are failing.

## Technique and Health

### Before playing

This is not stretching.

- Move shoulders, elbows, forearms, hands, neck, and torso gently.
- Breathe normally.
- Check bench height and distance.
- Begin with easy, quiet playing.

Warm-up means gentle movement and increased circulation. Stretching, if appropriate at all, comes later and is never forceful.

### Biomechanical check-in (every session)

Before and during practice, scan for:

- Wrist alignment: is it neutral or deviating?
- Shoulder tension: are shoulders elevated?
- Finger action: are fingers close to keys or lifting excessively?
- Forearm rotation: are arpeggios using rotation or finger stretching?
- Arm weight: is sound coming from weight or force?
- Breathing: are you holding your breath?

If any answer is wrong, stop and correct before continuing.

### Scales, arpeggios, and RCM Technical Requirements

Two distinct roles:

- As warm-up: a few minutes of slow, relaxed scales to get blood flowing and check for tension. No metronome, no pressure.
- As technique practice: deliberate work on the specific scales, arpeggios, chords, and dominant sevenths required at your current RCM level. This belongs in the technique slot, not the warm-up.

Apply the same principles as etude work: pick by objective, use the tempo protocol, and rotate focus across keys and patterns.

### During

- Pause every 20–30 min, or sooner if focus declines.
- Stop immediately for pain, weakness, tingling, numbness, swelling, loss of dexterity, unusual fatigue, or increasing tension.
- Do not force stretches, play through pain, or chase tempo with tension.
- Every 20 minutes, do a 10-second body scan: shoulders, wrists, fingers, breathing.

### After

- Gentle movement, not aggressive pulling.
- Persistent pain: see a professional familiar with musicians.
- Spend 1 minute shaking out hands and rolling shoulders.

### Piano-specific movement resources

- Thomas Mark, What Every Pianist Needs to Know About the Body
- Penelope Roskell, The Complete Pianist
- BAPAM's free Healthy Pianist resources
- Taubman Approach / Golandsky Institute
- Lister-Sink Method for injury prevention

## Biomechanical Practice Principles
>
> These principles apply to every piece, every exercise, every session.
> They are not optional. They are the foundation of sustainable technique.

### Principle 1: Efficient kinetic chain

The force path should be: shoulder → upper arm → forearm → wrist → fingertip.
Any break in this chain wastes energy and creates tension.

- Keep forearm, wrist, and fingertip in a straight line during key depression.
- Avoid ulnar deviation (wrist bending toward pinky).
- Avoid radial deviation (wrist bending toward thumb).
- The wrist should be a flexible joint, not a rigid one.

### Principle 2: Economy of motion

Less motion equals faster speed and less fatigue.

- Fingers should stay close to the keys.
- Do not lift fingers higher than necessary.
- For fast passages, minimize finger height.
- For large leaps, prepare the hand shape in the air, not on arrival.

### Principle 3: Weight, not force

Sound comes from arm weight transferred through the fingers, not from finger force.

- Drop the arm onto the key from a height of 1–2 inches.
- Feel the weight transfer through the fingertip.
- For louder playing, use more arm weight, not more finger force.
- For softer playing, use less weight, not less finger contact.

### Principle 4: Rotation for arpeggios and repeated patterns

Forearm rotation is more efficient than finger stretching for arpeggios, Alberti bass, and tremolos.

- Think of turning a doorknob or waving goodbye.
- The wrist and forearm rotate; the fingers stay relatively still.
- This prevents the common error of stretching the hand to reach arpeggio notes.

### Principle 5: Wrist as shock absorber for octaves and chords

The wrist must be flexible and springy for octave passages.

- Drop into the octave with a relaxed wrist.
- Let the wrist bounce slightly between octaves.
- Never lock the wrist rigid.
- For large chords, use the whole arm, not just the hand.

### Principle 6: Finger independence

Each finger should be able to move independently without dragging adjacent fingers. (This is NOT a finger 'independence' exercise)

- Practice holding one finger down while moving others.
- Practice polyrhythmic patterns between fingers.
- Practice voicing: play one note louder than the others in a chord.
- Use black keys to increase the challenge.

### Principle 7: Whole-body coordination

Piano playing is not just hands. It is the whole body.

- Sit tall with a straight but relaxed spine.
- Engage the core for stability during large dynamic shifts.
- Shoulders relaxed, not elevated.
- Feet flat on the floor for grounding.
- Breathing: never hold your breath. Breathe with the phrases.

### Principle 8: Tension release

Tension is the enemy. It must be identified and released. (Tension release doesn't mean playing sloppily)

- After each phrase, consciously release the shoulders and wrists.
- During rests, drop the hands to the lap and shake gently.
- If you feel tension building, stop and reset.
- Tension during practice becomes tension during performance.

## Mental Practice and Consolidation
>
> Mental practice is a legitimate training modality backed by neuroscience.
> It activates the same motor cortex regions as physical practice.
> It is especially effective for memorization, passage planning, and consolidation.

### When to use mental practice

- Travel days, rest days, injury days
- Before bed (sleep consolidation window)
- Between physical practice sessions
- When physically fatigued but mentally alert
- As a supplement after physical practice

### Mental practice protocol

1. **Choose a passage**: Pick 2–8 bars you are working on.
2. **Visualize**: Close your eyes. See the keys. See your hands. Feel the movements. Hear the sound. Be as specific as possible.
3. **Add detail**: Feel the key weight, the finger angles, the wrist motion. Hear the dynamics, the articulation, the tone.
4. **Slow down**: Visualize at half speed or slower. Every movement should be clear.
5. **Repeat**: Do 3–5 visualizations of the passage.
6. **Test**: Next time you sit at the piano, play the passage cold. Compare to your visualization.

### Score study away from the piano

- Read the score like a book. Follow the harmony.
- Mark the form: A, B, A', coda.
- Identify key changes, modulations, sequences.
- Sing each voice separately.
- Describe the piece in words: "This section moves from I to vi to IV to V."
- This builds analytical memory, which is the safety net when muscle memory fails.

### Sleep consolidation integration

- After your last physical repetition, spend 2–3 minutes visualizing the passage perfectly.
- Before bed, do one more 2-minute visualization of the day's most important passage.
- Sleep 7–9 hours. The brain replays motor sequences during slow-wave sleep.
- Expect 10–20% improvement overnight on the visualized passage.
- This is not magic. It is neuroscience.

## Dual-Task Training
>
> Dual-task training builds the cognitive capacity to manage multiple streams of information simultaneously.
> This is the core demand of sight-reading, ear-playing, and performance.
> Start simple. Increase difficulty only when the base task is at 80% accuracy.

### What it is

Playing a physical task while performing a cognitive task simultaneously.
This trains the brain to allocate attention efficiently, reducing interference between motor and cognitive systems.

### Progression ladder

| Level | Physical task | Cognitive task |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 1 | Play a scale slowly | Count aloud from 1 to 20 |
| 2 | Play a simple piece | Name the note letters as you play |
| 3 | Play a piece | Name the chord (I, IV, V, vi) |
| 4 | Play a piece | Count a different subdivision aloud |
| 5 | Play a piece | Conduct with the free hand |
| 6 | Play a piece | Analyze harmony aloud |
| 7 | Play a piece | Listen for a specific voice and name it |
| 8 | Play a piece | Sing a different voice |
| 9 | Play by ear | Name the chords while playing |
| 10 | Improvise | Describe what you are doing aloud |

### When to use

- During sight-reading practice: play while counting or naming chords
- During ear-training: play while singing or naming intervals
- During polishing: play while analyzing harmony or voicing
- During warm-up: play scales while counting or naming keys

### When NOT to use

- Day 1 of learning a new piece
- When the base task is below 80% accuracy
- When fatigued
- When the passage is still unclear

### Rules

- Start at the level where you can maintain both tasks at 80% accuracy.
- If either task drops below 60%, reduce difficulty.
- 3–5 minutes per session is enough.
- Do not use dual-task for speed work or technical drilling.
- Use it for reading, ear-playing, and performance preparation.

## Core Loop

Plan a specific result → attempt it → diagnose the limiting problem → choose the appropriate strategy → retest after a delay → perform under realistic conditions → maintain only what matters.

### Extended core loop with consolidation

1. Plan a specific result
2. Attempt it physically
3. Diagnose the limiting problem
4. Choose the appropriate strategy
5. Retest after a delay (Rule of Almost)
6. Visualize the passage before bed (sleep consolidation)
7. Test cold the next day
8. Perform under realistic conditions
9. Maintain only what matters
