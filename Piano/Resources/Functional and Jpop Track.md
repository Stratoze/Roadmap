# Functional and J-pop Track
>
> The parallel track for playing songs, lead sheets, and J-pop/anime music by ear and by chord.
> This is a different skill axis from the classical [Roadmap](Roadmap.md). It is graded by harmonic and comping fluency, not by repertoire difficulty.
> It lives in the Ear and Fun slots of the daily note. It is not primary technical training, but it is real training.
> End goal: play anime songs by ear at Animenz level, leveraging classical technique for expressive, complex arrangements.

## Why this track exists

- Classical study builds reading, touch, and repertoire depth.
- Functional study builds the ability to sit down and play a song you love, accompany a singer, harmonize a melody, and play from a chord chart.
- J-pop and anime music are a primary motivation for many players. Giving them a real progression keeps the fun slot from becoming aimless noodling.
- Both tracks share one practice engine: [Practice Protocols](Practice%20Protocols.md). Chunking, tempo control, error triage, and Rule of Almost all apply.
- The Animenz goal requires classical technique. The two tracks are not separate; they feed each other.

## Positioning

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |

| How much? | Minimum one 10–15 min functional session per week. More if it is your main motivation. |
| Does it match my classical stage? | No. Run it as an independent axis. A classical Stage 2 player may be functional F1. |
| Does it replace classical work? | No. Technique, reading, and repertoire still come from the classical track. |
| How does it connect to La Campanella? | Classical technique enables Animenz-level playing. The two tracks converge at Stage 6+. |

## The five skill axes

Functional fluency is five skills practiced in rotation, not one linear path.

1. Chord vocabulary — triads, sevenths, extensions, inversions.
2. Comping patterns — left-hand and two-hand rhythmic figures that realize a chord chart.
3. Lead-sheet reading — melody plus chord symbols into a realized performance.
4. By-ear — extracting melody, bass, and progression from a recording.
5. Arranging and improv — turning a song into a piano version, and varying it.

## The Animenz pathway

Animenz arrangements are the convergence point of classical technique and functional playing.
They require:

- Rapid arpeggios and broken chords (classical: Czerny, Chopin etudes)
- Hand independence and polyphony (classical: Bach, counterpoint)
- Octave passages and large chords (classical: Liszt, Rachmaninoff)
- Expressive voicing and pedaling (classical: Romantic repertoire)
- Harmonic sophistication (functional: jazz, city-pop vocabulary)
- Rhythmic drive and groove (functional: J-pop, anime)

### Animenz technical prerequisites by classical stage

| Classical stage | Animenz capability |
| ---: | --- |
| 3–4 | Play simplified Animenz arrangements with block chords and melody |
| 5 | Play intermediate Animenz arrangements with broken chords and basic arpeggios |
| 6 | Play advanced Animenz arrangements with octave passages and complex voicing |
| 7+ | Play full Animenz arrangements at performance level |
| 8+ | Transcribe and arrange anime songs independently at Animenz level |

### Animenz transcription workflow

1. Listen to the original song 3–5 times. Identify form, key, tempo.
2. Listen to the Animenz arrangement 3–5 times. Follow the score if available.
3. Identify the main progression and melodic hooks.
4. Transcribe the melody by ear. Sing it first.
5. Transcribe the bass line.
6. Identify the chord progression. Name each chord.
7. Work out the accompaniment pattern: arpeggio, broken chord, or block.
8. Add the intro, interlude, and outro.
9. Practice hands separately, then together.
10. Add dynamics, voicing, and pedaling.
11. Perform and record.

## Functional stage ladder

| Stage | Skill markers |
| ---: | --- |
| F0 Foundations | Major and minor triads. I–IV–V–I in a few keys. Single-note melody with a simple left-hand chord on beat 1. Read a very simple lead sheet. |
| F1 Basic comping | Triad inversions for smooth voice leading. The axis progression I–V–vi–IV. Block-chord and root–fifth comping. Play melody plus chords from a lead sheet in one key. |
| F2 J-pop entry | Seventh chords (maj7, m7, dom7). The royal road progression IV–V–iii–vi. Broken-chord and arpeggio patterns. Start songs on IV. Play a simple anime or Ghibli melody with chords. |
| F3 Arranging | Extended chords (add9, maj7, m7, sus). Secondary dominants and the ii–V–I turnaround. Ballad and 8th-note arpeggio patterns. Arrange a verse–chorus song with an intro. |
| F4 Color and style | Modal interchange and borrowed chords. Slash chords and pedal tones. Syncopated and push comping. City-pop voicings with extensions. Arrange a full song with intro, dynamics, and outro. |
| F5 Performance | Reharmonize a melody in two or more ways. Improvise over a form. Play a set of arrangements performance-ready. Recover and keep going. Transcribe Animenz arrangements independently. |

## J-pop and anime harmonic vocabulary

These are the sounds that make the style recognizable. Learn them by name, by ear, and under the fingers.

### The royal road progression (王道進行)

IV–V–iii–vi. In C: F–G–Em–Am.

- This is the signature J-pop and anime sound.
- It never settles on I, which creates forward longing.
- Common variant: IV–V–vi, which skips the iii.
- It appears in countless opening and ending themes. Train your ear to catch it.

### Other core progressions

| Progression | In C | Character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Axis | I–V–vi–IV | Bright, universal pop |
| Royal road | IV–V–iii–vi | Longing, bittersweet, very J-pop |
| Royal road variant | IV–V–vi | Shorter sigh |
| Driving minor | i–VI–III–VII | Epic, rock-leaning anime opening |
| ii–V–I turnaround | Dm7–G7–Cmaj7 | Jazzier, city pop |
| Ballad descent | I–V/7–vi–IV or falling bass | Power ballad, emotional |
| Chromatic mediant | I–bIII–bVI–bVII | Epic, cinematic anime |
| Pedal point | Static bass, moving chords | Underscore, atmospheric |

### Habits of the style

- Songs frequently begin on IV, not I.
- Chords are colored with add9, maj7, and m7 even in simple songs.
- Secondary dominants (V/V, V/vi) appear constantly.
- A static pedal tone under moving chords is common in anime underscore.
- Melodies are often chromatic against diatonic harmony.
- Modulation up a half step or whole step for the final chorus is common.
- Intro often uses a different progression from verse/chorus.

## Comping pattern progression

Choose patterns by the song's character. Practice each until automatic, then apply to real songs.

| Stage | Left-hand / two-hand patterns |
| ---: | --- |
| F0–F1 | Block chord on beat 1. Root–fifth. Root–octave. |
| F2 | Broken chord in quarter or eighth notes. Simple waltz bass. |
| F3 | Ballad pattern: root–octave then chord. Flowing 8th-note arpeggios. |
| F4 | Syncopated pushes and anticipations. 16th-note groove for uptempo. Slash-chord bass motion. |
| F5 | Full Animenz-style: multi-voice textures, counter-melodies, octave reinforcement, orchestral voicing. |

## Improvisation frameworks for ear-playing

Improvisation is not random. It uses vocabulary.

### Level 1: Melodic variation

- Play the melody, then vary it: add passing tones, neighbor tones, or rhythmic displacement.
- Change one note. Does it still work? Try another.

### Level 2: Harmonic variation

- Play the same melody over different chords.
- Reharmonize one chord in the progression.
- Add a ii–V before a target chord.

### Level 3: Textural variation

- Play the same song as a ballad, then uptempo, then in a different style.
- Change the comping pattern while keeping the melody.
- Add an intro or outro.

### Level 4: Free improvisation

- Improvise over a chord progression using the scale or arpeggio of each chord.
- Target chord tones on strong beats.
- Use call and response: play a 4-bar phrase, then answer it.
- Improvise in the style of a song you just learned.

### Level 5: Arranging as improvisation

- Take a song and create a full piano arrangement on the spot.
- Decide: key, tempo, form, intro, comping pattern, dynamics, outro.
- This is the Animenz skill: turning any song into a piano performance in real time.

## Repertoire by stage

Use licensed scores and official piano collections. See [Editions and Sources](Editions%20and%20Sources.md#anime-and-game-music) and [Resource List](Resource%20List.md#japanese-anime-and-game-music).

| Stage | Suggestions |
| ---: | --- |
| F1–F2 | Ghibli songs with simple charts: Tonari no Totoro, Sanpo, Kimi wo Nosete. Folk or children's songs for easy melody plus chords. |
| F2–F3 | Itsumo Nando Demo, Merry-Go-Round of Life, Ryuichi Sakamoto Energy Flow. Anime opening or ending themes with official piano scores. |
| F3–F4 | Joe Hisaishi piano collections. J-pop ballads. City-pop standards with extended chords. Begin Animenz simplified arrangements. |
| F4–F5 | Arrange a song you love from scratch: melody, bass, chords, intro, outro. Then reharmonize it. Full Animenz arrangements. |
| F5+ | Transcribe Animenz arrangements by ear. Create original arrangements. Perform a set of anime/J-pop pieces. |

Treat these as functional arrangements, not exam repertoire. The goal is to play them, not to perfect them for a grade.

## How to practice this track

Use the same engine as classical work, adapted.

- Pick one song as your current functional project. One is enough.
- Preview: listen to a recording, find the melody, name the key and the main progression.
- Chunk: work the intro, verse, and chorus as separate units.
- Hands: learn the melody and the chord roots first, then add the comping pattern.
- Tempo: use the [Adaptive Tempo Protocol](Practice%20Protocols.md#adaptive-tempo-protocol). Ballads slower than you think; uptempo songs start slow.
- End by playing the section as music, with the intended feel.
- Log it in your daily note. One journal line is enough.

### By-ear minimum

Once per week, spend 10 minutes pulling a short phrase by ear:

1. Find the melody note by note. Sing it first.
2. Find the bass or root of each chord.
3. Name the progression.
4. Play melody plus simple left-hand chords.
5. Add one variation: change the comping, add a passing tone, or reharmonize one chord.

### Arranging minimum

Once a month, take one song you know and make one small arranging decision on purpose:
change the voicing, add an intro, reharmonize one chord, or vary the comping on the second verse.

### Dual-task training for functional playing

- Play a chord progression while singing the melody
- Play the melody while naming the chords aloud
- Play bass notes while clapping the rhythm
- Play a song while counting subdivisions
- Play a song while a metronome plays on off-beats

## When to advance

Move to the next functional stage when you can:

- play the stage's chord vocabulary in two or three keys without pausing
- realize a lead sheet at that level into a continuous performance
- keep a steady groove through the comping pattern for that level
- play one song from the stage's list start to finish with intended feel
- demonstrate the improvisation skill for that level

Advancement is by fluency, not by time. A functional stage may take weeks or months.

## Convergence with classical track

At classical Stage 6+, the two tracks begin to merge:

- Classical technique (arpeggios, octaves, voicing) enables Animenz-level playing
- Functional harmony (sevenths, extensions, reharmonization) enriches classical interpretation
- Ear training from functional playing improves classical memorization
- Sight-reading from classical track enables reading complex Animenz scores
- The end state is a pianist who can play La Campanella AND sit down and play any anime song by ear
